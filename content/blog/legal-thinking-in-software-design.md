Law and software may look like very different fields, but both depend on structure. Each asks you to understand rules, identify exceptions, work with evidence, and explain a conclusion clearly.

## Requirements are a form of interpretation

A software requirement rarely answers every question. Developers have to interpret what it means, identify assumptions, and decide how the system should behave when the normal path changes.

Legal training encourages the same habits:

- define important terms;
- separate facts from assumptions;
- look for conflicting rules;
- consider edge cases; and
- explain the reasoning behind a decision.

## Applying this to case management

Case-management software has to represent information that changes over time. A matter may move through different stages, involve multiple people, and collect documents or events along the way.

Thinking carefully about states and transitions helps keep that information understandable. Clear labels and consistent rules are not just technical concerns—they affect whether users can rely on the system.

## A useful combination

My legal background influences how I approach software: pay attention to language, make logic visible, and avoid hiding important assumptions. Legal-technology projects give me a practical place to develop that combination further.
